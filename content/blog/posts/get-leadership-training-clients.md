Walk down a street of tailors in any Indian city and read the boards. Gents Tailor. Suits, Shirts, Alterations. Ladies and Gents. Suits, Shirts, Alterations. Twelve shops, one board. You pick the one nearest the parking, or the one who quotes the lowest, and you would not notice if two of them swapped premises overnight.

Then there is the one shop with a queue outside it in November. He does not do alterations. He does wedding sherwanis, and everyone in that part of the city knows it. He charges three times what the others charge, he is booked six weeks out, and nobody asks him for a discount, because nobody thinks of him as a tailor. They think of him as the sherwani man.

Leadership training in India is a street of tailors. Open the brochures of twenty firms and you will find the same board on all of them: communication, delegation, emotional intelligence, coaching skills, managing change, leading teams, strategic thinking, executive presence. Same list, same stock photographs, same promise to build leaders of tomorrow. The buyer picks the nearest or the cheapest, and the firms wonder why work only arrives through referrals and why every proposal ends in a haggle over the day rate.

This article is about how to get leadership training clients when the promise is this crowded. It sits under my wider guide to [marketing for corporate training companies](/marketing-for-training-companies), which covers who buys training and why good trainers get compared on price. Here I take the particular problem of leadership development: why it all sounds the same, how a firm finds a sharper position, how to package a programme a sponsor will trust, and the small weekly routine that brings the clients in while the facilitators are busy delivering.

## Why "Leadership Training" All Sounds the Same

The sameness is not laziness. It comes from a sensible-looking decision that almost every firm makes early on.

A new leadership development firm wants to be eligible for as much work as possible. So it lists everything it can teach. Any L&D manager who comes looking should find their topic on the page. The list grows with every programme delivered, and after five years the firm has a catalogue of forty modules and a brochure that says, in effect, we can do whatever you need.

Here is what that does to the buyer. An L&D head with a brief for a first-time-managers programme now has twenty firms who can all do it. They have no way to tell them apart from the brochures, because the brochures are the same. So they do the only thing a buyer can do with identical offers: they ask for a quote, line the quotes up, and either pick the lowest or pick the firm someone in their network has used before. That is why most leadership training firms live on referrals and renewals, and why the day rate keeps sliding. It is not a sales problem. It is a marketing problem that shows up at the sales stage.

The second reason is the word itself. "Leadership" is a label a buyer cannot test before purchase. A sales training firm can be judged on whether the numbers moved. A leadership firm is selling something that everyone agrees is important and nobody can quite define. In the absence of a way to judge quality, buyers judge on familiarity and price. The people typing "leadership training topics" into Google are often L&D managers doing exactly this: building a list of modules to compare vendors against. A firm that answers that search with yet another list of twelve topics has volunteered to be compared.

There is a third reason, and it is the uncomfortable one. Many firms genuinely do not have a point of view. They have facilitators, frameworks licensed from abroad, and a catalogue. Ask the founder what they believe about leadership that most of their competitors do not, and the answer is a long pause. A tailor who does everything has no reason to be chosen, and no way to be remembered.

## Finding a Sharper Position

![Three groups of leaders, with one chosen as the firm's position](/images/blog/get-leadership-training-clients/inline-1.webp)

The way out of the street of tailors is to become the sherwani man. In leadership development that means choosing, out loud, which leaders, which problem, and what you believe about fixing it. The general method is in [positioning for consulting firms](/blog/positioning-for-consulting-firms) and the wider case in [how to differentiate a B2B services firm](/blog/how-to-differentiate-b2b-services-firm). For a leadership training firm, the choice has three parts.

**Which leaders.** A first-time manager, a general manager running a plant, and a CXO team deciding strategy have almost nothing in common except the word leader. Each sits in a different budget, is sponsored by a different person, and fails in a different way. A firm that is clearly for one of them is instantly more credible than a firm that is for all three. Newly promoted managers in fast-growing companies is a position. Second-generation leaders taking over family businesses is a position. Plant heads in manufacturing is a position. "Leaders at all levels" is a brochure.

**Which problem.** Leadership is never the thing the sponsor actually wants. The CEO of a mid-sized firm who funds a leadership programme wants something more specific: the business to stop depending on them for every decision, the regional heads to run their regions without escalating, the next layer to be ready before the current layer retires. Name that problem and you have a reason to be chosen. [Scaling beyond the founder](/blog/scale-beyond-the-founder) is one of those problems, and it is a large one in India, where a great many businesses are run by one person who cannot let go. A leadership firm that owns that problem is in a different conversation from one that teaches delegation as module seven.

**What you believe.** This is the part firms skip, and it is the part that makes the position stick. A point of view is a claim about how leaders actually change, stated in a way that some competitors would disagree with. You might believe that leadership cannot be taught in a classroom and must be practised on live business problems. You might believe that most leadership programmes fail because they train the manager and leave the manager's boss untouched. You might believe that first-time managers in Indian companies fail for one specific reason, and you can name it. Any of those is a flag planted. A buyer who agrees will seek you out, and a buyer who disagrees was never going to be a good client.

I have watched this choice change a business. A trainer whose work in the room was excellent sold "communication skills", and every conversation with HR drifted or ended in a day-rate squeeze. We aimed the same material at senior leaders and renamed it for the outcome. Leadership development sits in a different budget from soft skills, with a senior sponsor and far less resistance. The engagements grew and the fees moved into a band the old name could not have supported, and he taught almost exactly what he had always taught. The story is in [from day-rate trainer to leadership budgets](/case-studies/cs-04-communications-trainer). The lesson cuts both ways. If a communication trainer can climb into the leadership budget by narrowing, a leadership firm can fall out of it by trying to be everything.

A note on the fear. Every founder I have had this conversation with worries that narrowing means turning work away. In practice it means the opposite. When you are known for first-time managers in fast-growing companies, you still get asked to run the CXO offsite, because you are the firm the buyer already trusts. The difference is that now they call you first, and they do not ask for three quotes.

## The 70-20-10 Rule, and What It Means for Your Offer

L&D buyers in India know the 70-20-10 model well, and it is worth understanding what it does to your sales conversation. The model says that roughly seventy per cent of how leaders develop comes from doing the job, twenty from other people, and ten from formal training. Whether the numbers are precise is beside the point. What matters is that your buyer believes the classroom is the smallest part.

Most leadership training firms sell the ten per cent. Two days in a hotel conference room, a workbook, a feedback form at the end. The buyer funds it because they have to show the board that leadership is being developed, and then watches the behaviour drift back within a month. This is why the question "why do employees forget training so quickly" is one of the most searched in the category. The buyer already suspects that the classroom alone does not work.

A firm that sells the other ninety per cent is in a different market. A programme that puts leaders on a live business problem for twelve weeks, pairs them with a senior sponsor, and brings their managers into the room for the review is not competing with a two-day workshop, and the buyer knows it. The classroom becomes one part of the design rather than the whole product. This is the single most useful reframe available to a leadership training firm, and it comes straight from a model your buyer already believes.

## Packaging a Programme Buyers Trust

Position chosen, the next question is what, exactly, the buyer can buy. A catalogue of modules is not an answer. A signature programme is.

A signature programme is one named piece of work, aimed at one group of leaders, built around one problem, with a defined shape and a defined outcome. It is the sherwani. It is what you are known for, what your case studies are about, and what the sponsor forwards upstairs. Everything else you can do still exists, but it is sold inside the trust the signature programme earns. [Offer architecture for a predictable lead engine](/blog/offer-architecture-predictable-lead-engine) covers the general shape; here is what it looks like for leadership development.

**Name it for the outcome, not the content.** "Leadership Essentials for Managers" describes content and invites comparison. "Ready to Run a Region" describes an outcome and invites a conversation. The name is often the only thing the sponsor sees before deciding whether to take the meeting, because it arrives in a forwarded email. It has to work without you there to explain it.

**Build the sponsor in.** The person who funds a leadership programme is almost never the person who attends it. Design the programme so the sponsor has a role: a kick-off where they set the business problem, a review where the cohort presents what they did, a conversation at ninety days about what changed. This does two things. It makes the programme work better, because the participants know someone senior is watching. And it gives you a relationship with the person who holds the budget for next year.

**Give it a shape the buyer can picture.** Three months, one cohort of twelve, four working sessions, a live project between them, a review with the leadership team at the end. A buyer can carry that upstairs in one sentence. "Forty modules, pick what you need" cannot be carried anywhere.

**Make the first step small.** A programme for twelve senior people is a large decision for a company that has never worked with you. Give the sponsor a smaller first purchase that proves you up close: a half-day diagnostic with the leadership team on where their next layer is weakest, or a single working session with one group of managers on a live problem. It is easy to approve, it tells you what the company's version of the problem really is, and it makes the programme that follows a natural next step rather than a leap. My [guide to getting corporate training clients](/blog/get-clients-for-corporate-training) covers the proposal L&D can forward upstairs, which matters just as much here.

**Put the proof of change inside the programme.** The feedback form on the last day measures whether people enjoyed the session. Nobody funds a second programme because the first one was enjoyable. Build a ninety-day check-in with participants and their managers into the design, asking what they now do differently. That gives you a story of change told by the sponsor, which is the only kind of proof that moves the next buyer. One such story is worth more than a page of logos.

## Executive Coaching: The Adjacent Market and Its Trap

![The classroom as the smallest part of how leaders develop](/images/blog/get-leadership-training-clients/inline-2.webp)

Many leadership training firms add executive coaching, and many coaches try to grow into leadership training. The two sit next to each other, and the marketing for them is different in a way worth spelling out.

Coaching is bought one leader at a time, usually by HR or the leader's boss, and the decision rests on trust in the individual coach. It is personal, slow to build and hard to delegate. Leadership training is bought for a cohort, by a sponsor, and the decision rests on the programme and the firm. Winning executive coaching clients is largely a matter of the coach being known and trusted by a few dozen senior HR people and CEOs, which is a founder-brand job. Winning leadership training clients is a firm-brand job, with the programme as the thing people remember. [Founder brand vs company brand](/blog/founder-brand-vs-company-brand) is about exactly this fork.

The trap is trying to be both without deciding. A firm whose founder is the only name anyone knows will find that every programme enquiry wants the founder in the room, and the firm cannot grow past their calendar. A coach who hides behind a firm name will find that nobody knows whom they are hiring. Decide which business you are in. If it is both, make coaching the small first step that leads into a programme, or make the programme the thing that produces coaching enquiries for the senior people in it. Either works. Drifting between them does not.

## Reaching the Sponsor, Not Just the Training Manager

Position, programme, proof. Now the firm has to be found by the right people, and most of its marketing has been aimed at the wrong ones.

L&D managers find vendors. They do not, usually, decide to spend on leadership development; they are asked to, by someone above them who has a business problem. The CEO who has decided the second line is not ready. The business head whose regional managers escalate everything. The HR head who has been told by the board that succession is a risk. These are the people who turn a general wish into a budget, and they rarely read training brochures.

They do read about their problem. A CEO worried that the business cannot run without them will read an article on exactly that. A business head whose managers will not take decisions will read a piece on why newly promoted managers freeze. This is where a point of view earns its keep. A firm that writes, plainly and regularly, about the specific leadership problem it has chosen will be found by the sponsor at the moment the sponsor is looking. A firm that posts photographs of workshops with the caption "another successful programme" will be found by nobody.

The channels are not exotic. **LinkedIn**, in the founder's or lead facilitator's own name, with one clear idea per post drawn from what you saw in the last room. [A LinkedIn content system that scales without burnout](/blog/linkedin-content-system-scales-without-burnout) covers how to keep that going for a firm that is always delivering. **A website** with one page per signature programme written for the sponsor rather than for L&D, naming the problem, the leaders it is for, the shape, and one story of change. **Search**, which is quieter but patient; sponsors type their problems into Google, and a clear written answer meets them there. [SEO for consultants](/blog/seo-for-consultants) applies to a training firm without alteration. **Small rooms**: a talk at a management association chapter or a CXO forum, in front of thirty business heads, is worth more than a stall at a national HR conference, for reasons my [guide to getting public speaking clients](/blog/get-public-speaking-clients) goes into.

Then there is the channel every leadership firm has and almost none use: the leaders they have already trained. A manager who went through your programme three years ago is now a business head somewhere else. They remember you, they have budget, and they have the same problem one level up. A short note twice a year, with something useful in it, keeps you the firm they call. Most firms let these people vanish into LinkedIn and then wonder why the pipeline depends on a handful of HR contacts.

## How to Get Leadership Training Clients Online

![A signature programme with the sponsor built in](/images/blog/get-leadership-training-clients/inline-3.webp)

People ask this as if the internet were a separate market. It is the same market with a longer memory, and it rewards exactly the firms that have done the positioning work above.

Online, a sponsor does three things. They look you up after hearing your name, to see whether you are what they were told. They search for their problem, and find you or do not. And they decide, usually within a minute of landing on your site, whether you are a vendor to be quoted or an expert to be met. Everything about the online presence of a leadership training firm should be built for those three moments.

That means the site leads with the problem and the programme, not with the catalogue and the client logos. It means the founder's LinkedIn reads like someone with a view on how leaders change, not a photo album of ballrooms. It means that when a sponsor searches for why first-time managers struggle in Indian companies, there is a clear, plain article in your name waiting for them. And it means that when someone responds to any of it, you write back within two days, because online attention fades fast. [From social media followers to clients](/blog/social-media-followers-to-clients) is about that last step, which is the one most firms miss.

What online does not do is replace trust. Nobody funds a three-month programme for their senior team because of a website. The website earns the first conversation. The diagnostic earns the programme.

## Pricing Past the Day Rate

The way leadership training is bought drags pricing towards the day rate, and the day rate is where undifferentiated firms go to die. A sponsor asks for a two-day workshop, the firm quotes per day, procurement lines it up against three other per-day quotes, and the one with the best brochure photography at the lowest number wins. The firm has volunteered to be compared on the one dimension where it cannot win against a freelancer with no overheads.

A signature programme changes the unit. It is priced per cohort, as one piece of work with a defined outcome: twelve leaders, three months, a live project, a review with the leadership team, a ninety-day check-in. Nothing in the other quotes looks like it, so there is nothing to line it up against. The price is attached to the change in the business rather than to the hours in the room. For how to work out what that number should be, [value-based pricing for consultants](/blog/value-based-pricing-for-consultants) is the place to start, and [what your rates tell prospects](/blog/pricing-signals-what-your-rates-tell-prospects) is worth reading before you decide to be the affordable option.

Buyers do ask how much leadership training costs, and the honest answer is that in India it runs from a few thousand rupees a head for an open workshop to programmes for a senior cohort costing several lakh. The range is so wide because the buyer is not buying one thing. They are buying a two-day event at one end and a change in how a company is run at the other. Which end a firm sits at is decided by its positioning and its packaging long before anyone writes a number.

## The Weekly Routine That Brings Clients In

The enemy of marketing for a leadership training firm is delivery. When the facilitators are in rooms four days a week, nobody is working the pipeline, and the quiet months arrive as a surprise every year. So the routine has to be small enough to survive a busy quarter, and it has to run even in the weeks when the calendar looks full.

- **One piece of writing a week**, in the founder's or lead facilitator's name, on the leadership problem you have chosen. Drawn from the last room, not from a content calendar.
- **Five short notes a week** to past participants, past sponsors and HR contacts who have moved companies. Something useful in each, no pitch.
- **One diagnostic proposed a month**, to a company that has heard you speak or read your work, as the small first step before a programme.
- **One small room a quarter**: a chapter meeting, a CXO forum, an executive education session. Chosen for who is in it, not for the fee.
- **One proof story a quarter**, written up from a programme with a ninety-day check-in, told in the sponsor's words.
- **One look at the pipeline a week**, twenty minutes, to see which conversations have gone quiet and why.

That is three to four hours a week. It does not depend on the founder being free, and it matters most in the months when the diary is full, because the enquiries it generates arrive a quarter later. ## What Marketing Consulting for a Leadership Training Firm Looks Like

![A past participant now senior in another company](/images/blog/get-leadership-training-clients/inline-4.webp)

Since it is the natural next question, here is how I work with leadership development firms.

I start with a diagnosis rather than a plan. The [Pipeline Reality Check](/pipeline-reality-check) is a one-week look at where your work really comes from: your last twenty engagements, who sponsored each one, how the buyer found you, which proposals went to a day-rate comparison, and which conversations drifted and why. It shows whether the constraint is marketing at all.

Sometimes it is not. If most of your diagnostics turn into programmes and the pipeline is full of the right sponsors, the answer is pricing or capacity, and I will say so.

When it is marketing, the work follows the [CLEAR method](/clear): which leaders and which problem the firm is for, a signature programme named for the outcome, a page a sponsor can forward upstairs, proof of change in public, and a weekly routine that runs while the facilitators deliver. Each engagement is scoped after a conversation, because a two-person firm with one founder's reputation and a twenty-facilitator firm with a licensed framework need quite different things.

## Questions Leadership Training Firms Ask Me

### How do I get leadership training clients?

Stop selling leadership training and start selling a specific change for a specific group of leaders. Choose which leaders, which problem, and what you believe about fixing it. Package that as one signature programme named for the outcome, with the sponsor built in and a small first step in front of it. Then write about the problem, in your own name, where the sponsors will find it, and follow up with everyone who responds within two days.

### How do I get leadership training clients online?

Build the site for the sponsor, not the training manager: the problem, the leaders it is for, the programme's shape, one story of change. Write regularly, in the founder's name, about the leadership problem you have chosen, so that a CEO searching for it finds you. Keep the catalogue off the home page. Online earns the first conversation; the diagnostic earns the programme.

### What is the fastest way to get leadership training clients?

The people you have already trained. A manager who went through your programme a few years ago is now senior somewhere else, with budget and the same problem one level up. A useful note to each of them, and a diagnostic offered to the ones who respond, produces conversations within weeks. Everything else, including search and LinkedIn, takes a quarter or two to start paying.

### How much does leadership training cost in India?

Anywhere from a few thousand rupees a head for an open workshop to several lakh for a programme with a senior cohort. The range is wide because the buyer is buying different things at each end: a two-day event, or a change in how the company runs. A firm decides which end it lives at through its positioning and packaging, long before it quotes.

### What is the 70-20-10 rule for leadership, and does it matter for my firm?

It is the model that says most leadership development happens on the job and through other people, with formal training the smallest part. Your buyers believe it. A firm that sells only the classroom is selling the ten per cent, and competing with every other classroom. A firm whose programme is built around live business problems, sponsors and managers is selling the other ninety, and that is a different, better market.

### Should a leadership training firm also offer executive coaching?

Only if you decide how the two fit. Coaching is bought on trust in an individual; training is bought on trust in a programme and a firm. Make coaching the small first step into a programme, or make the programme the source of coaching enquiries for the senior people in it. Offering both as separate catalogue items dilutes both.

## Find Your Weakest Link

The tailor with the queue outside did not become the sherwani man by adding sherwanis to his board. He became it by taking everything else off. Leadership training firms that struggle to win clients beyond referrals are almost never short of skill in the room. They are short of a position anyone can remember, a programme anyone can picture, and a routine that keeps the pipeline moving while the facilitators deliver. The work was never the problem. The business around it was.
