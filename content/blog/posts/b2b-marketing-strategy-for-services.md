A cricket captain has nine fielders and a ground far too big for them. He cannot cover every part of it, and he does not try. Before the over starts he looks at the bowler, looks at the batsman, and decides where the ball is likely to go. Then he leaves gaps on purpose. A packed off side and an empty fine leg is not carelessness. It is a decision.

That is what a marketing strategy is. Not a list of everything a firm could do, but a choice about where to stand, made before the work starts, with the gaps left open deliberately.

Most B2B services firms I meet have the opposite of this. They have activity: a LinkedIn page, a website refresh, a newsletter that went out twice, an event sponsorship someone suggested. Each one made sense on its own day. Together they cover the ground thinly, and the ball keeps going through.

This guide is about the decisions behind a B2B marketing strategy for a services firm: a consulting practice, an agency, a training company, a professional services firm. It covers what a strategy has to decide, how it differs from what product companies do, which channels earn their place, a worked example, and how to put the whole thing on one page.

## What Is a B2B Marketing Strategy?

B2B means business to business: your buyer is a company, not a consumer. In marketing terms that changes three things. The decision is made by more than one person, it takes weeks or months rather than minutes, and the buyer is spending someone else's money, so they need to be able to defend the choice to a boss or a board.

A B2B marketing strategy is the set of choices that decides how your firm will become known and trusted by those buyers. It answers who you are trying to reach, what you want them to believe about you, how you will prove it, where they will come across you, and what they can do next.

Notice what that definition leaves out. It does not include a content calendar, a list of platforms, or a budget split. Those are the plan, and the plan comes after. A strategy is what you decide; a plan is what you run. Firms that start with the plan end up running a lot of activity in service of a decision nobody made.

## Why a Services Firm Needs a Different Strategy

Most B2B marketing advice is written for software companies. Read twenty articles on B2B marketing strategies and you will find funnels, free trials, product demos, paid campaigns, and teams of five or more running them. Very little of it fits a firm of eight people selling expertise.

The difference is simple. A software buyer can try the product. A services buyer cannot try you. They are buying judgement, delivered later, by people they have not yet worked with. The only things they can inspect before signing are what you say, what others say about you, and how you think when they talk to you.

That shifts where the strategy does its work. For a product company, a large share of marketing is about getting the product in front of people. For a services firm, almost all of it is about trust. Your marketing has to answer three questions a buyer will not ask out loud: do you understand my problem, can I trust your judgement, and what would working with you actually be like?

It also explains a pattern I see often. A firm's sales look weak: proposals go quiet, prospects compare fees, every good month traces back to one or two referrers. The firm reads this as a sales problem and works on closing. But the sales conversation is where the marketing gap shows up, not where it starts. When a buyer arrives with no reason to prefer you, price is the only thing left to compare.

## The Five Decisions in a B2B Marketing Strategy

![Five decisions in a marketing strategy](/images/blog/b2b-marketing-strategy-for-services/inline-1.webp)

When I work through marketing strategy with a services firm, it comes down to five decisions. Each one depends on the one before it, so the order matters.

**1. Who you serve.** Not "SMEs" or "growing businesses". A buyer you could find on a list: the role, the industry, the size, and the moment when they need you. "Operations heads at mid-sized pharma manufacturers preparing for a regulatory audit" is a decision. "Businesses that want to grow" is not.

**2. What you want to be known for.** The problem you solve, named the way that buyer names it, and the one thing about how you solve it that others do not offer. This is positioning, and it is where most strategies quietly fail. I have written a full guide to [positioning for consulting firms](/blog/positioning-for-consulting-firms), and the words that carry it are covered in the [B2B messaging framework](/blog/b2b-messaging-framework).

**3. Your proof.** The evidence a stranger can check: case studies with the reasoning left in, specific client quotes, worked examples of a problem and how you thought about it. Most services firms have years of good work and almost none of it written down.

**4. Two channels.** Where your chosen buyer already looks when they have the problem. A small firm can sustain two channels properly; it cannot sustain six. More on choosing them below.

**5. A way in.** A small first step for a buyer who is interested but not ready for a full engagement: a short diagnostic, a workshop, a fixed-scope review. B2B services are large decisions, and asking for the large decision first often gets you no decision at all.

If you can write each of these in one sentence, you have a strategy. If any of them takes a paragraph of "it depends", that is the decision to make next, before spending anything on channels.

## Positioning Does Most of the Work

Of the five, the second does most of the work, which is why I spend the most time on it.

A sharp position makes every later decision easier. If you know you help family-owned manufacturers professionalise before the next generation takes over, you know which events to attend, which articles to write, which case studies to tell, and which enquiries to politely decline. A vague position makes every later decision harder, because everything is equally relevant, so nothing is.

There is a test I use. Take your homepage headline and put a competitor's name on it. If it still reads as true, you do not have a position yet; you have a description of your category. Most services firm websites fail this test in the first line.

The fear behind a vague position is always the same: if we narrow down, we will lose business. In my experience the opposite happens. A narrow position does not stop other buyers from calling. It just gives the right ones a reason to call you first. When I [differentiate a B2B services firm](/blog/how-to-differentiate-b2b-services-firm), the narrowing is usually what brings the enquiry volume up, not down.

## A B2B Content Marketing Strategy: One Idea a Week

![One idea, reused across channels](/images/blog/b2b-marketing-strategy-for-services/inline-2.webp)

For a services firm, content is the main way to answer the three silent questions at scale. It is also the part that collapses first when client work gets busy.

The strategy here is not a content calendar. It is one decision: what point of view you will publish, again and again, from different angles. A content marketing strategy for B2B services works when every piece argues some part of the same position. It fails when each piece is about whatever was trending that week.

The routine that survives a busy firm is small. One idea a week, written properly, drawn from a question a client actually asked. Then that one idea reused: a LinkedIn post, a paragraph in your next proposal, an opening question for a sales conversation. One idea used four times beats four ideas used once.

What makes it work is the thinking, not the volume. I cover that difference in the guide to [thought leadership for services firms](/blog/thought-leadership-for-services-firms). The short version: if nothing you publish is a position a reader could disagree with, it is filling space, not building trust.

## Which Platform Works Best for B2B Marketing?

People ask this expecting one name. For B2B services in India the honest answer is: the one your chosen buyer already uses when they have your problem. That is why the channel decision comes fourth, not first.

That said, a few patterns hold for most firms of one to fifty people.

**LinkedIn** is where most Indian B2B buyers already spend some of their working day. It reaches people quickly and forgets them quickly. It works best as the place where your written ideas get a second life, not as a separate production line.

**Your own website and articles** are slow to start and last for years. A good article keeps answering a buyer's question long after you wrote it, and it gives every other channel something to point at.

**Rooms where buyers gather**: industry associations, chambers of commerce, sector conferences, alumni groups. One talk to forty people who share your client's problem is worth more than a month of posts. Few firms do it, which is part of why it works.

**Your existing network, used deliberately.** Past clients and the people who have referred you before. A short note when you publish something useful keeps you in mind. [Referrals are not the problem](/blog/stop-relying-on-referrals); depending on them alone is.

**Careful direct outreach.** Ten thoughtful notes a week to firms that clearly have the problem you solve. The guide to [cold outreach for consultants](/blog/cold-outreach-for-consultants) covers how to do it without becoming a nuisance.

Choose two. Run them for a year. Paid advertising, directory listings, and trend commentary all have their uses, but not as the first thing a small firm spends its limited hours on.

## A B2B Marketing Strategy Example

![A marketing strategy on one page](/images/blog/b2b-marketing-strategy-for-services/inline-3.webp)

Here is an illustrative example, built from patterns I see often rather than any one client. Take a twelve-person HR consulting firm in Pune. Its work comes almost entirely from the founder's network, and it describes itself as offering "end-to-end HR solutions for growing businesses".

Run it through the five decisions.

- **Who:** founders and HR heads of manufacturing companies with 200 to 1,000 employees, typically facing their first serious attrition problem on the shop floor.
- **Known for:** reducing shop-floor attrition through supervisor training and structured onboarding, rather than broad "HR transformation".
- **Proof:** three past engagements written up as short case studies, each with the starting attrition problem, what was changed, and what the client said a year later.
- **Two channels:** a monthly article on shop-floor retention, reused as LinkedIn posts; and talks at two regional manufacturing associations a year.
- **Way in:** a two-week attrition diagnostic for one plant, with a written report.

Nothing in that list is expensive. Nothing needs a marketing team. What it needs is the founder's willingness to stop describing the firm as able to do everything, which is usually the hardest part. The rest is a routine.

## How to Create a B2B Marketing Strategy on One Page

You do not need a deck. You need one page, and an afternoon with the people who sell and deliver the work.

1. **List your best five clients from the last three years.** Best means profitable, good to work with, and where your work clearly made a difference. Look for what they share: industry, size, the problem they came with, who made the decision.
2. **Write the five decisions in one sentence each.** Who, known for, proof, two channels, way in. Use the client list as evidence, not your hopes.
3. **Check the position against your homepage.** Apply the competitor's-name test. Rewrite the first screen if it fails.
4. **Gather the proof you already have.** Pick the three engagements that best show your position and write them up.
5. **Set the weekly routine.** One idea a week, its reuses, and a handful of conversations. Put it in the calendar like a client meeting.
6. **Decide what you will measure.** Where enquiries come from, how many fit the buyer you chose, and how first calls begin. Review it every quarter.

That is the strategy. ## How Do You Attract B2B Customers?

By being the obvious choice for a specific problem, before the buyer starts looking. B2B buyers in services rarely search for a firm cold. They notice someone over months: an article a colleague forwarded, a talk at an association meeting, a name that came up twice. When the problem becomes urgent, they call the firm they already associate with it.

So attracting B2B customers is less about any single campaign and more about being consistently present, on one clear topic, in the few places your buyers already look. That is what the five decisions set up and what the weekly routine keeps going. Expect six to twelve months before it produces enquiries reliably. The early signs, prospects quoting your ideas back to you, show up sooner.
