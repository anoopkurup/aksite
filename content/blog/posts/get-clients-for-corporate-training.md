Every Indian town has one. A restaurant with a board outside that reads: Pure Veg. South Indian, North Indian, Chinese, Continental, Pizza. Inside, the menu runs to twelve laminated pages. The food is usually fine. The kitchen is clean, the staff are pleasant, and the dosa is perfectly good.

But nobody drives across town for it. When a family wants a really good biryani, they go to the place that only makes biryani, even if it is further away, even if the chairs are plastic and there is a queue. The multi-cuisine place can cook a biryani too. That is not the point. Nobody believes a kitchen that cooks everything is the best at any one thing.

Most corporate training firms in India are multi-cuisine restaurants. Their website lists thirty or forty programmes: communication skills, leadership, time management, negotiation, sales, customer service, emotional intelligence, team building. Each one is competently designed and competently delivered. And when an L&D head needs someone to fix one particular problem, the firm with the longest menu is rarely the one they think of first.

This article is my answer to the question of how to get corporate training clients. It starts a step before the usual list of tactics, with what a buyer understands about you before they ever reply. It sits under my wider guide to [marketing for corporate training companies](/marketing-for-training-companies), which covers who buys training and why good trainers get compared on day rate. Here I go further into the firm with many programmes and many trainers: how buyers actually decide, how a programme gets approved, and how the firm grows from the accounts it already has.

## Why a Long Training Catalogue Loses

A training firm builds its catalogue for good reasons. A client asked for a negotiation module, so the firm built one. A trainer joined who was strong on customer service, so that went on the list. An L&D head said they would book more if the firm also did time management, so it did. Each addition made sense on the day. Ten years later, the firm has forty programmes and no single thing it is known for.

The catalogue feels like safety. More programmes, more chances to be picked. In practice it works the other way, for three reasons.

**A buyer remembers one thing about you, at most.** An L&D head deals with dozens of training vendors. When a sales head walks in and says "my new managers cannot run a team meeting", the L&D head reaches for a name. The name that comes to mind is the one that stands for that problem. A firm that stands for forty things stands for none of them in particular.

**A long list reads as a generalist, and generalists get compared on price.** If your leadership programme looks like every other leadership programme, the only thing left to compare is the fee. The catalogue makes this worse, because it tells the buyer you are a supplier of training in general, much as a stationery shop is a supplier of pens.

**The catalogue spreads your proof thin.** A firm that has run one programme two hundred times has stories, patterns and results it can talk about with real authority. A firm that has run forty programmes five times each has a little proof about everything and convincing proof about nothing.

The sales symptoms will be familiar. "Please send us your training calendar." "Can you share your profile and a few modules?" A quote request for a two-day programme, followed by a counter-offer. Repeat bookings from the same three accounts, and very few new ones. Firms respond by adding programmes, sending more proposals and chasing harder. The problem is not in the follow-up. It is in what the buyer has already decided about you.

## How Corporate Training Clients Actually Decide

It helps to watch a training decision from the inside, because the moment that matters is earlier than most firms think.

A training need rarely starts in L&D. It starts with a business head who has noticed something. Attrition among new hires is up. The sales team keeps losing deals at the proposal stage. A round of promotions has created twenty first-time managers who are struggling. The business head mentions it to HR, often in a corridor or at the end of a review.

L&D then does one of two things. If a name comes to mind straight away, they call that firm. If not, they send a short brief to the three or four vendors they already use, and perhaps ask a colleague in another company who they would recommend. In either case, the shortlist is built from memory. Very few L&D heads search the open market for every requirement. They do not have the time.

This is why a firm that is not remembered for a specific problem loses before the race starts. By the time the brief goes out, the shortlist has been drawn up, and the firm that was on it was the one the L&D head already associated with that problem.

The calendar adds a second layer. Many Indian companies plan training budgets in the last quarter of the financial year, from January to March, and then run programmes between April and December. A firm that only markets when its trainers have a gap in the diary is visible at the wrong time. The decisions about next year's programmes are being made while the firm is busiest delivering this year's.

So the question of how to get corporate training clients becomes a more useful question: how do you become the name that comes to mind when a particular problem is mentioned, and how do you stay in mind through the months when budgets are drawn up?

## The Note L&D Has to Write

![A training proposal turned into a single page that L&D can carry upstairs](/images/blog/get-clients-for-corporate-training/inline-1.webp)

There is a step in most training decisions that the training firm never sees, and it decides more engagements than the proposal does.

After L&D has picked a programme, someone has to approve it. Usually that means L&D writing a short note to the CHRO, the business head or the finance team, explaining why this spend is worth it. In a large company, that note may pass through two or three people. The trainer is not in that meeting. L&D has to argue the case alone, in their own words, using whatever the training firm gave them.

Look at what most firms give them. A brochure of modules. Learning objectives written in the language of competency frameworks. A trainer's profile listing certifications. A rate card. Nothing in that pile helps L&D answer the only question the approver will ask: what will be different afterwards, and how will we know?

So the programme is judged on what is left, which is the fee and the dates.

The firms I have seen win steadily do something simple. They write the approval note for L&D. Not literally, and not in a way that does L&D's job for them, but they shape every proposal so it can be forwarded upstairs as it stands. It answers five questions on one page:

- **The problem**, in the business head's words, not in training language. "New managers are losing their best people in the first year."
- **The group**: exactly who attends, and why them.
- **What changes**: what participants will do differently at work, described as behaviour a manager could observe.
- **How we will know**: what will be checked, by whom, and when, after the programme ends.
- **The first step**: what happens before the full programme is committed to, such as a short needs assessment or one pilot cohort.

A proposal built like this does two things at once. It makes L&D look good to their own bosses, which is what L&D most wants from a vendor. And it moves the conversation away from the day rate, because the approver is now reading about a business problem being solved, not a training expense being incurred.

This is packaging, and it is a marketing job, not a sales one. The work is done once, when you decide how a programme is described, and then every proposal benefits.

## From Catalogue to a Signature Programme

![Many training programmes in a list, with one lifted out as the programme the firm leads with](/images/blog/get-clients-for-corporate-training/inline-2.webp)

The way out of the multi-cuisine trap is not to throw away the menu. It is to decide what the restaurant is famous for, and put that on the board outside.

For a training firm, that means choosing one or two signature programmes to lead with. These are the programmes you name on your website's first screen, write about every week, build your proof around and bring up first in every conversation. The rest of the catalogue stays available to clients who ask. It just stops being the way strangers meet you.

How do you choose? I look for three things together.

**A problem a business head owns.** Not a topic L&D files under soft skills, but a problem someone senior feels and will put their name behind. First-time managers who lose people. Sales teams that cannot handle procurement. Senior leaders who cannot present a plan to a board. The closer the programme is to a business result, the easier it is to sponsor.

**The place where your proof is strongest.** Which programme have you run most often, with the clearest results, for the most recognisable kinds of company? That is usually where your real authority lies, even if it is not the programme you enjoy most.

**A group with a budget.** Programmes for the broad middle of an organisation sit in the thinnest budgets and face the hardest approvals. Programmes aimed at leaders, at revenue teams or at people in their first leadership role usually sit in budgets that are easier to approve. I saw this clearly with a communication trainer whose work moved into a different budget, and a different fee, once it was aimed at senior leaders and renamed for the outcome. The story is in [from communication skills to speak like a leader](/case-studies/cs-04-communications-trainer).

Then give the signature programme a name that describes the outcome, not the topic. "Business Communication Skills" describes an activity. "The First Ninety Days as a Manager" describes a situation the buyer recognises at once. The name is often the first thing a business head hears, forwarded in an email by L&D. It has to work without explanation.

Choosing feels risky, because it seems to turn work away. In my experience it does the opposite. A firm known for one thing gets asked about other things by clients who already trust it. A firm known for nothing has to win every account from scratch. I go into the general case in [positioning for consulting firms](/blog/positioning-for-consulting-firms) and [how to differentiate a B2B services firm](/blog/how-to-differentiate-b2b-services-firm); both apply to training firms almost word for word.

## Reaching the Problem-Owner, Not Just L&D

Once you know which problem you stand for, you know whom to reach. It is the person who owns that problem, and they are usually not in L&D.

This does not mean going around L&D. That rarely ends well, and L&D will remember it. It means being known to business heads as well, so that when they mention the problem to HR, your name is already attached to it. L&D then gets a request to call you, which is the easiest kind of brief to act on.

Three places reach the problem-owner better than any brochure.

**The sponsor debrief.** At the end of most programmes, the trainer reports back to L&D. Ask for twenty minutes with the business head who sponsored it as well. Tell them what you saw in the room: the patterns, the gaps, what their managers struggled with. This is the most useful conversation you can have with a buyer, and most training firms never ask for it.

**Past participants who have moved up.** A manager who sat in your programme four years ago may now head a business unit, in the same company or a new one. They have a budget, the same problem you helped with before, and a memory of you. Most firms lose touch with participants on the last day. Staying in occasional, useful contact with them is slow work, and it compounds.

**Rooms where business heads gather.** Industry association chapters, management association events, CXO roundtables. A short talk on the problem you are known for, followed by a conversation with two or three people who came up afterwards, does more than a stall at an HR conference. The talk is a sample of the product. Treat it as the start of a conversation, not a performance.

## Proof Beyond the Feedback Form

![A feedback score that fades after the training day, and one that is checked again weeks later](/images/blog/get-clients-for-corporate-training/inline-3.webp)

Almost every training firm collects feedback forms at the end of a programme. Participants rate the trainer, the content and the venue. The average is usually somewhere above four out of five. Firms put these scores in proposals as proof.

They prove very little. A feedback form measures how participants felt at four in the afternoon on the last day. It says nothing about whether anyone behaved differently a month later. Business heads know this, which is why "4.7 average rating" does not move them.

There is a reason buyers are sceptical. It is often said that people forget most of what they learn in a training session within days unless they use it. The exact figure is debated, but every L&D head has seen the pattern: an energetic programme, warm feedback, and three weeks later, nothing has changed on the floor.

This is a problem for training firms, and also an opening. If forgetting is what buyers fear, then proof that the change lasted is the most persuasive thing you can show. And the way to get that proof is to build follow-up into the programme itself:

- **A check-in thirty or sixty days after**, with participants and with their managers, about what they are doing differently.
- **Small practice tasks** between sessions, carried out in real work and reported back.
- **A short conversation with the sponsor** after the check-in, about what they have noticed.

This makes the programme better, which matters in itself. It also produces the raw material for proof of change: what was happening before, what participants did differently, what the sponsor saw. Written up as a short story, without naming the client unless they agree, one of these does more for your next proposal than a stack of feedback forms. The same idea runs through [the praise trap](/blog/praise-trap-admired-but-not-paid): being liked and being paid well are not the same thing, and proof of change is what connects them.

## How to Get Corporate Training Clients Online

Owners often ask me how to get corporate training clients online, as if there were a separate set of tactics for the internet. Mostly there is not. Online channels make the work above visible to more people. They do not replace it.

**LinkedIn** is the main one for B2B training in India. It is where L&D heads and business leaders look you up before they reply. What works is the lead trainer posting in their own name, about the signature problem, drawing on what they see in rooms: the mistake every new manager makes in their first review, the question that unsettles a sales team in a negotiation. Photographs of full training rooms and certificates tell a buyer you were busy. They do not tell them you were good. The hub article on [marketing for corporate training companies](/marketing-for-training-companies) covers the kind of content that works, and [a LinkedIn content system that scales without burnout](/blog/linkedin-content-system-scales-without-burnout) covers how to keep it going.

**Search** is quieter but useful. Business heads type their problems into Google: why new managers fail, how to train a sales team to handle procurement. A firm that has written a clear answer to its signature problem meets buyers at the moment they are looking. The numbers are small, which suits a training firm that needs a few dozen good clients a year. [SEO for consultants](/blog/seo-for-consultants) covers the approach.

**A short open session for problem-owners** works better than a free demo for L&D. Ninety minutes on the signature problem, aimed at business heads, run online a few times a year. It is a sample of how you think, offered to the people who own the problem, and it gives you a reason to follow up with each of them afterwards.

**Vendor portals and training marketplaces** are worth less than they appear. Being one of four hundred listed trainers is the multi-cuisine menu again, with more pages.

## Pricing Past the Day Rate

Most corporate training firms price by the trainer-day. It is the convention, buyers expect it, and it is easy to quote. It is also the reason so many firms feel underpaid.

A day rate invites the buyer to treat your work as a unit of time. Units of time get compared, negotiated and trimmed. Once a programme is framed as "two days at a rate", the obvious move for procurement is to ask for one and a half days, or a lower rate, or both.

A signature programme makes a different pricing conversation possible. If the programme includes a needs assessment at the start, the sessions themselves, practice between sessions, a check-in with participants and managers at sixty days, and a debrief with the sponsor, then it is no longer a number of days. It is a defined piece of work aimed at a defined result, and it can be priced per cohort or per programme. The buyer can still compare it, but there is nothing quite like it to compare it with.

None of this needs a dramatic jump in fees. It needs the fee to be attached to the result rather than to the hours. How to work out what that should be is in [value-based pricing for consultants](/blog/value-based-pricing-for-consultants), and [what your rates tell prospects](/blog/pricing-signals-what-your-rates-tell-prospects) covers the signal a price sends before anyone reads the proposal.

## Growing Inside the Accounts You Already Have

![One training account branching out into other departments of the same company](/images/blog/get-clients-for-corporate-training/inline-4.webp)

Here is a pattern I see in almost every training firm I look at. The firm runs a programme for one department of a large company. It goes well. The firm is booked again by the same department the following year. And it never reaches the four other departments of that company, which have the same problem and the same budget cycle, a few floors away.

For most training firms, the easiest new client is not a new company. It is a different part of a company that already trusts them. The procurement work is done, the vendor code exists, L&D already knows your name. What is missing is a deliberate attempt to be introduced.

A simple way to start:

- **Map each account.** For your five largest clients, list the business units, the heads of each, and which ones you have worked with. Most firms find they have touched one or two of eight.
- **Ask the sponsor for the introduction.** A business head who saw results in their own team is the most credible voice you will ever have. At the debrief, ask who else in the company is facing the same problem.
- **Bring the proof story from one department to the next.** "Your colleagues in the sales team had the same issue with new managers, and here is what changed" is a very different opening from a cold proposal.
- **Track the people, not just the company.** When a sponsor moves to another company, they take their memory of your work with them. That move is often your next account.

This is unglamorous work. It does not show up in a social media feed. It is also where steady training firms find most of their growth, and it is the opposite of the habit of [relying on referrals](/blog/stop-relying-on-referrals) and hoping: the same relationships, worked on purpose.

## A Weekly Routine for a Firm That Is Always Delivering

The biggest enemy of marketing in a training firm is delivery. When the trainers are in rooms, nobody is marketing, and the busy season is exactly when next year's budgets are being drawn up.

So keep the routine small enough to survive the busiest month, and give it to someone who is not on stage that day:

- **One post a week** in the lead trainer's name, about the signature problem.
- **One sponsor debrief** requested after every programme that ends.
- **Five short notes a week** to past participants, past sponsors and L&D contacts who have moved companies.
- **One account map reviewed each week**, with one introduction asked for.
- **One proof story a quarter**, written up from a programme with a check-in.

That is two to three hours a week. It looks too small to matter. It matters because it keeps running in October, when the trainers are fully booked and most firms go quiet. ## What Marketing Consulting for a Corporate Training Firm Looks Like

Since it is the obvious next question, here is how I work with training firms. I run [workshops](/workshops) myself, so I know the odd economics of a business whose product is someone's time in a room.

I start with a diagnosis rather than a plan. The [Pipeline Reality Check](/pipeline-reality-check) is a one-week look at where your work really comes from: your last twenty engagements, which programmes they were for, who sponsored them, and which conversations drifted and why. It shows whether the constraint is marketing at all.

Sometimes it is not. If most of your proposals convert and your trainers are fully booked, the answer is pricing or hiring, and I will say so.

When it is marketing, the work follows the [CLEAR method](/clear): which problem and which group the firm leads with, a signature programme named for the outcome, a proposal L&D can forward upstairs, proof of change in public, and a weekly routine your team can run without me. Each engagement is scoped after a conversation, because a solo trainer and a firm with thirty facilitators need different things.

## Questions Training Firm Owners Ask Me

### How do I get corporate training clients?

Become known for one problem, for one group of people, before you try to be found. Most corporate training clients in India come from L&D heads who already know a firm, past participants who have moved up, and business heads who have heard the trainer speak. A signature programme named for its outcome, proof that behaviour changed, and a proposal L&D can forward upstairs make each of those sources work harder.

### How do I get corporate training clients online?

Use LinkedIn and search to make your signature problem visible, not to list your catalogue. Post in the lead trainer's own name about what you see in rooms, write clear answers to the problems business heads search for, and run a short open session for problem-owners a few times a year. Training marketplaces and directories rarely help, because they put you back among hundreds of similar listings.

### Is vendor empanelment worth the effort?

It is necessary to get paid by many large companies, so do it when an account asks. But being on an approved list rarely decides who gets chosen. A list of forty vendors is where L&D goes when no name comes to mind. The goal is to be the name that comes to mind.

### Why do employees forget training so quickly, and should a training firm care?

People forget what they do not use, and most programmes end on the last day with nothing to make participants use what they learnt. A training firm should care for two reasons: it makes the programme less effective, and it is what makes buyers sceptical. Building practice and a follow-up check-in into the programme improves the result and gives you proof of change to show the next buyer.

### How long does it take to win a new corporate training client?

Longer than most firms plan for. Because budgets are often set months before programmes run, a conversation that starts in October may lead to work in the following year's first or second quarter. This is why marketing has to keep running through the delivery season. The firm that is visible only when it has free days is visible at the wrong time.

## Good in the Room, Lost in the Catalogue?

The multi-cuisine restaurant is not a bad restaurant. It is a good kitchen that has made itself impossible to recommend. Corporate training firms are often in the same position: capable trainers, warm feedback, a long list of programmes, and a pipeline that depends on the same three accounts. The way out is to decide what you are famous for, make it easy for L&D to argue your case upstairs, show that the change lasted, and grow deliberately inside the companies that already trust you.
