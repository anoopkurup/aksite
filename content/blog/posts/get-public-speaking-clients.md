Walk into any good sweet shop before Diwali and someone behind the counter will hand you a piece of kaju katli on a square of paper. You did not ask for it. It is very good. And nobody at that shop thinks the sample is the business. The sample exists so that you buy a kilo, and then a kilo again at Holi, and then the boxes for the office.

A talk is a sample. Most B2B speakers and communication trainers in India treat it as the product.

The pattern looks like this. A twenty-minute slot at an industry conference. Three hundred people in the room, a warm round of applause, forty LinkedIn connection requests before dinner, and two people who come up afterwards to say the session was the best of the day. Then nothing. A month later the speaker is on another stage, for another token fee, with the same result. The calendar is full. The pipeline is empty.

This article is my answer to the question of how to get public speaking clients, for speakers and communication trainers who sell to companies rather than to the public. It sits under my wider guide to [marketing for corporate training companies](/marketing-for-training-companies), which covers who buys training and why good trainers get compared on day rate. Here I go into the particular problem of the stage: why applause does not turn into engagements, who is actually buying when a company books you, and how to build the business that the talk is supposed to be a sample of.

## Why B2B Speaking Is a Different Business From Keynotes

Search for how to get speaking clients and you will find a wall of advice for motivational speakers: build a showreel, join a bureau, apply to open calls, work the TEDx circuit, raise your fee as your name grows. That is a real business, but it is a different one. It sells a personality to an audience, and the money comes from ticketed events, book sales and a small number of celebrity-grade keynote fees.

A B2B speaker or a communication trainer sells something else. The audience is not the buyer. The buyer is a company, and what it wants is a change in how its people perform: a sales team that can present to a board, managers who can run a difficult conversation, senior leaders who can hold a room. The talk is how the company first meets you. The engagement that follows is the business.

This distinction decides almost everything about how you market. The motivational speaker needs to be famous. The B2B speaker needs to be known, to a few hundred of the right people, for one problem they can solve. The first takes a decade and a good deal of luck. The second is a marketing job, and it can be done on purpose.

The symptoms of mixing the two up are familiar to anyone who has tried. Event organisers who ask for your profile and your fee, and then explain that their budget covers travel and a memento. Work that only ever comes from someone who happened to be in the audience. The same two companies booking you every year while nobody new appears. These feel like sales problems, as if the fix were to chase harder or pitch more organisers. They are marketing problems. By the time an organiser asks for your fee, they have already decided what you are: a slot to fill. The work is to change what they decide before they ask.

## How to Get Paid Speaking Gigs, and Why It Is the Wrong Goal

![A small event budget beside a much larger company budget](/images/blog/get-public-speaking-clients/inline-1.webp)

I will answer the question people actually type, because it is a fair one: how do you get paid speaking gigs in India?

The honest answer is that the paid gig market is small and the fees are low, and that this is structural rather than a sign that you are not good enough. An event organiser buys a slot in a programme. The budget for that slot sits inside an events budget, which is one of the smaller budgets in any company, and it is competing with the venue, the catering and the sponsor's demands. Most industry conferences in India fill their stages with people who will speak for free, because speakers want the exposure and sponsors want the visibility. A few well-known names command a proper fee. Everyone else is negotiating against free.

So you can get paid gigs. You do it the way the general advice says: a clear topic, a short video of you speaking, a one-page profile, a list of past events, and a lot of asking. It works, slowly, and it caps out quickly. A speaker who depends on gig fees is a sweet shop living off the price of samples.

The useful move is to change what the gig is for. A paid gig is pleasant. An unpaid gig in front of the right forty people, with a plan for what happens afterwards, is worth more. Once you see the talk as a sample, the question of whether it was paid becomes secondary to the question of who was in the room and what you did in the week after.

That is not an argument for speaking anywhere for free. It is an argument for choosing stages by the audience, not the fee, and for treating a talk with no follow-up as a cost rather than as marketing. The hub article covers this trap for [training firms generally](/marketing-for-training-companies): a speaking slot that leads to no conversation is a performance, not marketing.

## What a Company Is Actually Buying When It Books You

Here is the thing that took me longest to see, watching communication trainers struggle to sell work that was clearly good.

A company never buys a talk. It never buys communication skills either. What it buys is a problem taken off someone senior's desk. The head of sales has a team that goes quiet in front of a procurement committee. The CHRO has twenty newly promoted managers who cannot give feedback without either a fight or a fudge. The CEO has a leadership team that cannot explain the strategy to the floor. Each of those people has a budget, a reason to act and a name they will put on the request.

Now look at how most speakers and communication trainers describe themselves. "Public speaking and communication skills trainer." "Keynote speaker on effective communication." That describes an activity, and it files you in the buyer's mind under soft skills: a generic programme aimed at the broad middle of the organisation, where budgets are thinnest and approvals hardest.

I saw this decide the fortunes of a trainer whose work in the room was genuinely excellent. The feedback was warm, the conversations with HR drifted, and when a programme did close it was at a day rate far below his worth. His craft was not the problem. His category was. We aimed the programme at senior leaders instead of the middle, and renamed it for the outcome: Speak Like a Leader. Leadership development sits in a different budget, with a senior sponsor and far less resistance when HR carries it upstairs. The conversations started closing, the engagements grew, and the fees moved into a band the old name could never have supported. He taught almost exactly what he had always taught. The full story is in [from communication skills to speak like a leader](/case-studies/cs-04-communications-trainer).

The lesson for a speaker is direct. Before you work on getting more stages, decide which problem, for which group of people, your talk is a sample of. A talk on "communication skills" is a sample of nothing in particular. A talk on why good sales teams lose in the last meeting with procurement is a sample of a programme a head of sales will fund. The general case of this choice is in [positioning for consulting firms](/blog/positioning-for-consulting-firms), and it applies to speakers word for word.

## Turning a Talk Into a Corporate Engagement

Once the talk is a sample of a specific thing, the work after the talk becomes obvious. Most speakers do none of it, because the talk felt like the finish line.

I think about it as the two-people rule. In any room of a hundred, there are perhaps two people who have the problem your talk described, own a budget for it and were paying real attention. The other ninety-eight enjoyed the session. Your job in the week after is to find the two and have a proper conversation with them. Everything else is noise.

Some of this happens before you go on stage. Ask the organiser who is in the room: which companies, which roles. If the audience is entirely HR executives at the junior level, and your programme is for sales leaders, that is a stage to decline or to treat as practice. If three heads of sales are attending, you know whom you are speaking to, and you can shape the talk around what they face.

During the talk, say what you do, once, in a sentence that names the problem. Not "I run communication workshops" but "I work with sales teams that keep losing in the final meeting with procurement, and I will be around afterwards if that is you". It is not a pitch. It is a label on the sample, so that the right person knows which counter to come to. The same principle runs through [what to say at a networking event](/blog/pitch-networking-events-lead-with-problem): lead with the problem, not the service.

After the talk, the mechanics are simple and they have to be done within two days, while the memory is warm:

- **Write to the two people** who came up afterwards, or whom you identified from the list. Not a thank-you note. A short message that names the thing they mentioned, adds one observation you did not have time for on stage, and offers a thirty-minute conversation about their team.
- **Write to the organiser** with the same offer for their own company, if it fits. The person who booked you has seen the sample closer than anyone.
- **Write one post** in your own name about the question that came up most in the room. Tag nobody. Say what you saw. The people who were there will recognise it, and the people who were not will see what the talk was about.
- **Record who was in the room** against your signature problem, so that the next time you have something to say on it, you know whom to send it to.

That is two hours of work after every talk. It is the difference between a speaker with a full calendar and a speaker with a pipeline, and it is the part almost nobody does, because the applause already felt like the reward. [The praise trap](/blog/praise-trap-admired-but-not-paid) is about exactly that feeling: being admired and being paid are not the same thing, and the gap between them is a system, not a talent.

## Packaging Communication Training Behind the Stage

![Two listeners in a large audience, marked as the ones to follow up with](/images/blog/get-public-speaking-clients/inline-2.webp)

The two people have agreed to a conversation. Now comes the question that catches most speakers out: what, exactly, can they buy?

If the only thing behind the stage is another talk, the conversation goes nowhere useful. The head of sales does not want a keynote for her team. She wants the team to stop losing. So the offer behind the stage has to be a programme that changes behaviour, and it has to be described in a way she can carry upstairs.

I think of it as a short ladder, with three rungs:

**The talk.** Forty-five to ninety minutes, free or for a fee, at an industry event or inside a company at a sales kick-off, a town hall or a leadership offsite. This is the sample. Its job is to make the problem vivid and to show how you think about it.

**The working session.** A half-day or one-day session with one team, on their real material: their actual proposals, their next board presentation, the difficult conversation a manager has been avoiding. This is the small first step, easy to approve, and it is where you learn what the company's version of the problem really is. Most corporate buyers will fund this before they fund a programme, because it is low risk and it answers the question of whether you are as good up close as you were on stage.

**The programme.** A cohort of senior people, worked with over weeks rather than hours, with practice between sessions and a check-in with their managers afterwards. This is the business. It is named for the outcome, aimed at a group with a sponsor and a budget, and priced as a programme rather than as days.

Each rung should lead naturally to the next, and the names matter at every level. "Presentation Skills Workshop" is a line item that someone in finance is paid to trim. "The Last Meeting: Winning in Front of Procurement" is a problem a head of sales recognises. The name is often the first thing the sponsor hears, forwarded in an email, so it has to work without you in the room to explain it. I go into the general shape of this ladder in [offer architecture for a predictable lead engine](/blog/offer-architecture-predictable-lead-engine), and my [guide to getting corporate training clients](/blog/get-clients-for-corporate-training) covers the proposal L&D can forward upstairs, which applies to speakers exactly as it does to a training firm.

One more thing about packaging. Build the proof of change into the programme. A check-in with participants and their managers sixty days later, asking what they do differently, gives you something no feedback form can: a story of what changed, told by the sponsor. One of those stories does more for your next conversation than any number of standing ovations.

## Where Corporate Speaking Work Comes From in India

It helps to be concrete about the stages worth standing on, because the speaking circuit in India is wide and most of it is the wrong room.

**Industry associations and chambers.** The sector bodies and regional chambers run chapter meetings, annual conferences and CXO forums all year. The national stages are crowded and hard to reach. The chapter meetings are not, and they put you in a room with twenty business heads from one city, which is a far better room than a national hall of five hundred strangers.

**Management associations and CXO forums.** Smaller rooms, senior people, and an audience that is there to think rather than to network. A talk here is close to a working session already.

**Inside companies.** The sales kick-off, the annual leadership meet, the town hall, the offsite. These are the best stages of all, because the audience is the buyer's own team and the sponsor is in the room. They are also the hardest to get onto from outside, which is why the first talk at an association chapter matters: it is how a business head decides to bring you in for their own kick-off.

**Executive education.** Business schools and their executive programmes use outside practitioners, and the participants are mid-career managers from companies that fund training. The fee is modest. The room is exactly right.

**Podcasts and webinars.** A long-form conversation on someone else's channel reaches people who would never attend a conference, and it leaves a recording you can send to a prospect for years.

**Speaker bureaus and open calls.** Useful once you are already known, and mostly a waste of effort before that. A bureau sells names, and the open-call circuit fills stages with free speakers. Neither solves the problem of being known for one thing.

Notice that the best stages are small. A B2B speaker is not trying to reach everyone. They are trying to be the obvious name, to a few hundred senior people, for one problem. That is a different kind of visibility from fame, and it is a great deal easier to earn.

## How to Get Public Speaking Clients Online

![Three offers stepping up from a talk to a full programme](/images/blog/get-public-speaking-clients/inline-3.webp)

People ask me how to get public speaking clients online as if the internet were a separate stage. Mostly it is the same stage, with a longer memory. Online channels let the right people find you, check you, and remember you between talks. They do not replace the work above.

**LinkedIn** is where a business head looks you up after the talk, or before replying to your note. What they find should match what they heard. The strongest LinkedIn presence for a speaker is not a feed of stage photographs and thank-you posts to organisers. It is the speaker writing, in their own name, about the problem: the mistake every sales team makes in the final meeting, what a leader's first town hall usually gets wrong, the question that came up in last week's room. A short clip from a talk, with one idea in it, works well. A full recording works less well, because nobody watches forty minutes on their phone. [A LinkedIn content system that scales without burnout](/blog/linkedin-content-system-scales-without-burnout) covers how to keep this going, and [from social media followers to clients](/blog/social-media-followers-to-clients) covers the step most speakers miss, which is what happens after someone has noticed you.

**Your own site** needs one page that does the job a showreel cannot. Most speaker pages are built for event organisers: topics, a video, a list of logos, a booking form. Build the page for the sponsor instead. Name the problem, name who it is for, describe the ladder from talk to programme, and show one story of what changed. The video can stay. It is proof you can hold a room, which is necessary and not sufficient.

**Search** is quieter but patient. Business heads type their problems into Google: why sales teams lose to procurement, how to prepare a leadership team for a town hall. A clear written answer meets them at the moment they are looking, and for a speaker who needs a few dozen good conversations a year, small numbers are enough. [SEO for consultants](/blog/seo-for-consultants) covers the approach.

**A short open session**, run online a few times a year for problem-owners, is the online version of the talk. Ninety minutes on the signature problem, aimed at the people who own it. It gives you a reason to write to each attendee afterwards, which is the point.

## Pricing: The Honorarium and the Programme

The way most speakers price is a direct result of the way they are bought. An organiser offers an honorarium, the speaker accepts or negotiates, and the number becomes the speaker's idea of their worth. Then a company asks for a one-day workshop, and the speaker multiplies.

This keeps a speaker stuck at the price of samples. A day of your time in a company's training room is compared with every other trainer's day rate, and procurement is paid to trim it. The ladder above changes the conversation. A talk can be free or carry an honorarium; it is a sample, and I would rather a speaker choose the stage by its audience than haggle over the fee. The working session carries a fee attached to its result: a team's real proposals reworked, a board presentation rebuilt. The programme is priced as a programme, per cohort, including the practice between sessions and the follow-up with managers. It is a defined piece of work aimed at a defined change in a group of senior people, and there is nothing quite like it to compare it with.

None of this requires a dramatic jump in fees on the day you decide. It requires the fee to be attached to the outcome rather than to the hours, and the buyer to be the person who owns the outcome. How to work out what the number should be is in [value-based pricing for consultants](/blog/value-based-pricing-for-consultants), and [what your rates tell prospects](/blog/pricing-signals-what-your-rates-tell-prospects) covers the signal a price sends before anyone reads a proposal.

## The Pipeline Behind the Stage

The enemy of marketing for a speaker is the same as for any trainer: delivery. When you are on stage every week, nobody is working the pipeline, and the stage is where you feel most alive. So the routine has to be small enough to survive a month of travel, and it has to be done even in the weeks when the calendar looks healthy.

- **Two follow-up notes within two days** of every talk, to the people who had the problem.
- **One post a week** in your own name, on the signature problem, drawn from what you saw in the last room.
- **One stage a month chosen by the audience**, not the fee. Decline the rest, or treat them as practice.
- **One working session proposed a month**, to a company that has heard you speak.
- **Five short notes a week** to past participants, past sponsors and organisers who have moved companies. The sponsor of a session three years ago is now a business head somewhere else, with the same problem and a memory of you.
- **One proof story a quarter**, written up from a programme with a sixty-day check-in.

That is three hours a week, and it does not depend on being on a stage that week. It matters most in the quiet months, when most speakers wait for the next invitation. ## What Marketing Consulting for a Speaker Looks Like

![A speaker page that leads with the problem it solves, not a showreel](/images/blog/get-public-speaking-clients/inline-4.webp)

Since it is the obvious next question, here is how I work with speakers and communication trainers. I run [workshops](/workshops) myself and have given my share of talks at industry events, so I know the pull of the stage and the strange economics of a business whose product is an hour of someone's attention.

I start with a diagnosis rather than a plan. The [Pipeline Reality Check](/pipeline-reality-check) is a one-week look at where your work really comes from: your last twenty engagements, which talks they came from, who sponsored them, who was in the room, and which conversations drifted and why. It shows whether the constraint is marketing at all.

Sometimes it is not. If most of your working sessions turn into programmes and the calendar is full of the right rooms, the answer is pricing or capacity, and I will say so.

When it is marketing, the work follows the [CLEAR method](/clear): which problem and which group the talk is a sample of, a programme named for the outcome, a page a sponsor can forward upstairs, proof of change in public, and a follow-up routine that runs in the week after every talk without needing you to feel like it. Each engagement is scoped after a conversation, because a speaker with a single signature talk and a communication training firm with six facilitators need different things.

## Questions Speakers and Communication Trainers Ask Me

### How do I get paid to speak?

Build a clear topic, a short video, a one-page profile and a list of past stages, and ask organisers directly. It works, slowly, and the fees in India stay modest because most conferences fill their stages with people who speak for free. The better question is what the talk is a sample of. A speaker with a programme behind the stage can afford to choose stages by the audience, and the money comes from the engagements, not the honorarium.

### How do I get into paid public speaking in India?

Start with the small rooms rather than the big stages: industry association chapters, management associations, executive education and internal company events. They are easier to reach, the audience is senior, and a talk there is how a business head decides to bring you into their own sales kick-off or leadership meet. Paid keynotes at national conferences come later, if at all, and they are rarely where the business is.

### How do I get public speaking clients online?

Write in your own name about the problem your talk solves, not about the talks themselves. Post short clips with one idea each rather than full recordings. Build a speaker page for the sponsor, not the event organiser: the problem, who it is for, the ladder from talk to programme, one story of what changed. And follow up with everyone who responds within two days, because online attention fades as quickly as applause.

### How much do keynote speakers get paid in India?

It ranges from a memento and travel to substantial fees for a small number of well-known names, and most working speakers sit at the lower end for structural reasons: the event budget is small and the supply of free speakers is large. A B2B speaker who earns well usually earns it from the programmes the talks lead to, where the buyer is a company with a leadership budget rather than an organiser with an events budget.

### Do I need a speaker bureau?

Not until you are already known for something, and perhaps not then. A bureau sells names it can place easily, which means names that organisers already recognise. For a B2B speaker, being known to a few hundred senior people for one problem does more than being listed alongside two hundred other speakers. If a bureau approaches you after that, take the call.

## Applause in the Room, Silence in the Inbox?

The sweet shop that hands you kaju katli at the counter is not being generous. It is being clear about what the sample is for. Speakers and communication trainers who struggle to turn stages into engagements usually have the opposite problem: a good sample, handed out to the wrong rooms, with nothing clearly for sale behind the counter and nobody following up when a customer shows interest. The talk was never the problem. The business behind it was missing.
