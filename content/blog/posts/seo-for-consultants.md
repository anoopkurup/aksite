Most consultants have a blog the way most people have a gym membership. It was set up with good intentions, it was used with real energy for about six weeks, and it now sits there quietly costing money and producing guilt. Eleven posts, the last one dated two years ago, none of them ranking for anything, and a nagging feeling that "content marketing" was supposed to do more than this.

I have been on both sides of this. I have built content operations for brands that published daily, and I now run a one-person consulting practice where the writing has to earn its keep without a team behind it. The second situation taught me more about SEO than the first, because when you cannot outproduce anyone, you have to outthink them. That is what SEO for consultants actually is: a small number of pages, chosen carefully, written by someone who knows the subject better than the people ranking today.

This is the low-drama version. No content calendar with forty entries, no keyword tool subscription you will cancel in month three. Just what works for an expert with a limited number of hours a week.

## Why Most Consultant Blogs Go Nowhere

A consultant's blog usually fails for one of three reasons, and none of them is a lack of writing talent.

The first is that it was written for peers. A tax consultant writes about the latest amendment in the language of other tax consultants. It gets appreciative nods on LinkedIn from people who will never hire him, and nothing from the business owner in Pune who typed "do I need to register for GST as a consultant" into Google at eleven at night. Peers admire; buyers search. The blog was optimised for the wrong audience, and it was never really optimised at all.

The second is that it was written for volume. Somebody said "publish consistently", so the consultant published whatever came to mind on a Thursday. Thirty posts on thirty unrelated subjects. Google sees a site with no centre of gravity, no page that is clearly the authority on anything, and treats it accordingly. Thirty mediocre pages rank worse than five good ones on a single subject, and they take six times as long to write.

The third is impatience. SEO is a slow channel. A well-built page takes three to nine months to settle into its ranking, and most consultants have stopped writing by month four. They then conclude that SEO does not work for consultants, which is a bit like concluding that the gym does not work after six weeks of going twice.

Notice that all three are marketing problems, not writing problems. Wrong audience is a positioning problem. Wrong subject is a strategy problem. Wrong timeline is an expectation problem. Fix those and the writing takes care of itself.

## Writing for Buyers, Not for Volume

![Three reasons a consultant blog goes nowhere](/images/blog/seo-for-consultants/inline-1.webp)

The reframe that fixes most of this is simple. Stop asking "what should I write about?" and start asking "what does my next client type into Google in the month before they hire someone like me?"

That question has a small number of answers, and you already know most of them. They are the questions prospects ask you in the first call. They are the objections you have answered a hundred times. They are the "should we do X or Y" decisions that a firm wrestles with before it admits it needs help. An HR consultant hears "how do we build a performance appraisal system for a 40-person company" every quarter. An IP consultant, which I once was, hears "is my idea patentable in India" every week. Each of those is a search, and each search is a page.

Write the list. Twenty questions is plenty; ten is a fine start. Then check each one for two things: does anyone actually search it (a free tool or even Google's own autocomplete will tell you), and does the person searching it look like a buyer? "SEO consultant salary" gets plenty of searches and every one of them is someone looking for a job. "Is my idea patentable in India" gets fewer searches and a good share of them are people with money and a decision to make. You want the second kind. Lower volume, higher intent, less competition, because everyone else is chasing the big number.

This is the 80/20 rule in SEO as it applies to a consultant: a handful of buyer-intent pages will bring most of the enquiries that ever come from search. The rest of the blog is optional.

## The Pillar-and-Spoke Structure (Meta: This Very Site)

Once you have the list, the pages need a shape, and the shape that works is one broad page surrounded by narrow ones.

The broad page is the pillar. It covers the whole subject at a level a buyer can follow: what the problem is, what the options are, how to think about it, where to start. It is long, it is the page you would send a prospect who asked "where do I begin", and it links out to every narrower page. The narrow pages are the spokes. Each answers one specific question properly, in depth, and links back up to the pillar and sideways to its neighbours.

You are reading a spoke right now. The pillar it hangs off is my guide to building a [lead generation system for a service business](/client-acquisition-system). Its neighbours include pieces on [cold outreach for consultants](/blog/cold-outreach-for-consultants) and on [where AI genuinely helps with getting clients](/blog/ai-for-client-acquisition). I did not write these in the order they appear; I wrote the pillar first, then the spokes in order of how often the question comes up in my own sales conversations. Every page points at the others, so a reader who lands on any one of them can find the rest, and so can Google.

Why this works is not mysterious. Search engines rank sites that demonstrate depth on a subject over sites that skim many subjects. Ten linked pages on one problem read as expertise. Ten unlinked pages on ten problems read as a diary. The structure is also kind to a solo writer, because it tells you exactly what to write next and when you are done. For a consultant, one pillar and eight to twelve spokes is a complete SEO operation. It will take a year. That is fine.

## Turning Expertise Into Rankable Pages

![Buyer intent beats search volume](/images/blog/seo-for-consultants/inline-2.webp)

Here is the part where consultants have an unfair advantage and mostly fail to use it. You have done the work. The agencies and content mills you are competing with for a ranking have not. Their page on "how to price a consulting engagement" is a summary of other pages on the same subject. Yours can contain the actual conversation you had last month with a client who was underpricing by half, what you told them, and what happened.

Google's own guidance now says plainly that it wants content showing first-hand experience. So does a buyer. A page with a real example, a real number, a real mistake and what it cost, will outrank a smoother page that has none of those, and it will convert better once it does, because the reader can tell that a person who has done the thing wrote it.

Practically, a rankable page from an expert looks like this. It answers the question in the title within the first two paragraphs; do not make a reader scroll past 400 words of preamble to find out whether their idea is patentable. It uses the words a buyer uses, in the headings, so that someone scanning can see their question answered. It runs to whatever length the question needs, which is usually longer than you think and shorter than the 4,000-word monsters ranking today. It includes at least one thing that only you could have written. And it ends by telling the reader what to do next, which for a consultant is normally a conversation, not a newsletter.

One more thing, since it comes up in nearly every conversation now: yes, you can use AI to help write these pages, and no, it cannot write them for you. ChatGPT is a good editor, a good outliner, and a decent researcher of what already ranks. It has no experience of its own, and experience is the only thing that makes your page different from the 200 others on the subject. Let it tidy your draft. Do not let it replace the part where you know something.

## How Long It Really Takes

![A pillar page surrounded by linked spokes](/images/blog/seo-for-consultants/inline-3.webp)

Consultants ask me two questions about SEO timelines and I will answer both honestly.

The first is "how long until I rank?" For a new site with modest authority, expect a well-built spoke to start showing up for its target phrase in three to six months and to settle wherever it is going to settle in nine to twelve. A pillar takes longer because it competes for broader terms. If your site is older and already has some links, the numbers shrink. If you are in a crowded field like general marketing consulting, they grow. Nothing you do in week one changes this much; the age of the page is a ranking factor you cannot rush.

The second is "how long will it take me?" This is the more useful question. One good spoke is four to eight hours of a consultant's time if they write it themselves from real experience, and longer if they try to research it from scratch, which is the wrong way round. At one page a fortnight, a full pillar-and-spoke set is a year of Saturdays. At one page a month, two years. Both are acceptable, because unlike outreach or advertising, the pages keep working after you stop paying attention to them. A spoke I wrote three years ago still sends me a qualified enquiry most months. Nothing else in marketing compounds like that.

Which is also the answer to "is SEO dead now with AI?" AI summaries in search results have reduced clicks for generic informational queries, the "what is X" type, where a machine answer is good enough. They have done far less to buyer-intent queries, where the searcher wants a person, a firm, and a next step. Those queries are the only ones a consultant should ever have cared about. If anything, the AI summaries clear out the low-value traffic and leave the enquiries. And the AI answers themselves are drawn from pages that demonstrate real expertise, which is one more reason to write the page nobody else could.

## Can I Do SEO Myself?

Yes, and for a consultant you probably should, at least for the writing. The technical side of SEO for a small site is now mostly handled by the platform you publish on; if the site loads quickly, works on a phone, and has sensible titles, you are most of the way there. The rest is choosing the right questions and answering them better than anyone else, and nobody can do that part for you, because nobody else has your client conversations.

Where help is worth paying for is upstream. If you cannot say clearly who you serve and why they should choose you, no amount of SEO will fix the page, because the page will be vague in exactly the way your positioning is vague. That is a marketing problem wearing an SEO costume, and I see it far more often than I see a genuine technical one.

## Traffic Is Not the Point

A consultant's site does not need traffic. It needs the right forty people a month to read a page that answers their exact question, decide the author knows what he is doing, and get in touch. Everything in this article is aimed at that, and none of it is aimed at a bigger number in an analytics dashboard.

Traffic without a system does not convert. The pages are one part of a lead generation system; the rest is a clear offer for the reader to find at the end of the page, a way to reply within a day, and a sales process that does not leak the enquiry once it arrives.
