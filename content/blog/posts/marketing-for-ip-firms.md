A ghostwriter's best book has someone else's name on the cover. That is the deal, and a good ghostwriter accepts it. The trouble starts when the ghostwriter needs the next client, and realises that the finest thing they have ever written cannot be shown to anyone.

That is the position most IP services firms are in. I mean the private limited companies that do the analytical and documentation work behind the patent system: prior-art and patentability searches, patent drafting, patent landscapes, invalidity and freedom-to-operate searches, portfolio analysis, technical documentation. They do not give legal opinions. The law firm or the in-house counsel does that. The IP firm does the work the opinion stands on, and usually does it under a client's name.

I ran an IPR firm for years, and for a long stretch it grew the way these firms usually grow. Clients arrived through introductions, the work was good, and I took that as proof of quality. A slow quarter taught me what it was actually proof of: I had no way to make a client appear. I could only wait for one.

This article is about marketing for IP firms of that kind: how they get known when the work is confidential, how they escape being compared on price per search, and how they build a pipeline that does not depend on who happens to mention them. It is also a description of work I now do, so read it with that in mind.

## What IP Buyers Actually Evaluate

Start with who buys, because it is rarely one person.

An IP services firm usually sells to a handful of buyer types: IP managers and R&D heads at technology companies, patent attorneys and law firms who send out searches and drafting, university technology transfer offices, and, for many Indian firms, corporations and law firms abroad. Each of them is buying the same thing underneath the service. They are buying work they can rely on without redoing it.

That shapes what they judge you on. In my experience they look for three things, roughly in this order.

**Do you understand my technology?** A pharmaceutical company wants searchers who can read a chemistry claim, not generalists who search everything. A medical device team wants a drafter who understands the device before they describe it. Technical depth is the first filter, and most IP firms hide theirs behind a generic list of services.

**Will I have to check your work?** This is the real question behind every outsourcing decision. A search that misses the key prior art, or a draft the attorney has to rewrite, costs the buyer more than doing it themselves. So they look for proxies: who else uses you, how clearly you report, whether your analysis shows judgement or just a pile of results.

**Is it predictable?** Turnaround, scope, cost, format. An IP manager with a portfolio review due next month does not want surprises.

Now look at a typical IP firm's website. Patent search, patent drafting, patent landscaping, patent analytics, IP support services. Often it is the same list, in the same order, as the next twenty firms. It answers none of the three questions, so the buyer falls back on the only thing left to compare: the price per search.

## Your Best Work Carries Someone Else's Name

This is the problem that makes marketing an IP services firm different from marketing almost anything else.

Your drafting is filed under the law firm's name. Your landscape goes into the client's board deck as the client's analysis. Your search report supports an opinion signed by an attorney you may never meet. Confidentiality agreements forbid you from naming most of your clients, and some of them would rather nobody knew the work was outsourced at all.

So the usual proof does not exist. You cannot publish a case study about the drafting you did for a global company's flagship patent. You cannot put the logos of the law firms you work with on your website. The firm is excellent and invisible at the same time.

There are three ways around this, and a good IP firm uses all three.

**Show the method instead of the client.** You cannot show the search you did for a client. You can show how you search: how you build a query strategy for a crowded technology, how you decide when a search is complete, what a useful search report looks like. Buyers are really trying to judge your method anyway.

**Publish work nobody commissioned.** A patent landscape of a technology you know well, built entirely from public data, is proof you can show anyone. Pick a field your buyers care about, map who is filing what and where, and say what you see. It demonstrates the exact skill you sell, in public, without breaking a single agreement.

**Ask for permission, once, in writing.** Some clients will agree to be named, or to an anonymised description of the work, if you ask properly after a project goes well. Most firms never ask.

## Building Trust Before the Request Arrives

![A firm visible only when a request arrives, versus one present throughout the buyer's year](/images/blog/marketing-for-ip-firms/inline-1.webp)

The second problem is timing. Most IP firms exist in a buyer's mind only when a request is already on the table: a search needed by Friday, a batch of drafts, a landscape for a board meeting. At that moment the buyer goes to whoever is already on the vendor list, or whoever a colleague mentions. The rest of the year, the firm is not in the picture.

The fix is to be useful before the request. That means knowing the moments when your buyers need you and turning up ahead of them:

- **A new R&D programme.** The best time for a landscape or a patentability search is before the budget is spent, not after.
- **A product launch or a new market.** Freedom-to-operate searches matter most before launch, and the teams planning launches rarely think of them early enough.
- **A funding round or an acquisition.** Someone will need to understand what a company's portfolio actually covers.
- **An annual portfolio review.** Many companies pay to maintain patents nobody has looked at in years.

Each of these is a moment you can see coming. Write about them, speak about them at the industry bodies, incubators and technology conferences where these buyers gather, and build relationships with the people who see those moments first: patent attorneys, IP managers, technology transfer officers, investors. A patent attorney who trusts your searches will send you work for years, and so will their colleagues when they move firms.

Then there is search, the Google kind. When I ran my firm, "is my idea patentable in India" was a question someone asked almost every week. It gets far fewer searches than a broad term, but a good share of the people typing it have a real invention and a decision ahead of them. Every question your buyers ask in the first call is a page waiting to be written. I explain how to find and write those pages in [SEO for consultants](/blog/seo-for-consultants).

## Positioning Past "Full-Service IP Support"

"End-to-end IP services" tells a buyer nothing. It is a menu, and nobody picks a restaurant because the menu is long.

An IP firm can narrow in two useful ways. The first is **by technology**: "patent search and drafting for medical devices", "landscapes for battery and energy storage", "chemistry and pharmaceutical searches". Technology is how buyers already sort the problem in their heads, and it is where your team's degrees and experience actually show. The second is **by buyer**: "overflow drafting for patent attorneys", "portfolio analysis for R&D heads". Each tells a stranger in a few words whether you are for them.

Narrowing feels like turning work away. It is the opposite. You can still take the odd project outside your focus. What you are choosing is what you are known for, and a firm known for one thing gets referred for that thing far more often than a firm known for everything gets referred for anything. There is more on this in [positioning for consulting firms](/blog/positioning-for-consulting-firms) and [how to differentiate a B2B services firm](/blog/how-to-differentiate-b2b-services-firm).

## Packaging IP Work So a Stranger Can Buy It

![Open-ended work turned into a small defined first engagement](/images/blog/marketing-for-ip-firms/inline-2.webp)

The third problem is that a new buyer has no safe way to try you. Outsourcing IP work is a risk: if your search misses something, the buyer's name is on the result. So a buyer who has never used you tends to stay with the vendor they already know, even an average one.

The answer is a small, defined first engagement with a clear scope and a fixed deliverable. For example:

- **A pilot search** on a problem the buyer has already searched themselves, so they can compare your results with what they know.
- **A one-technology landscape snapshot**: who is filing, where, and what has changed in the last three years, delivered in a set number of pages.
- **A trial draft** of a single application for a patent attorney, turned around in an agreed time.

Each one answers a question the buyer already has. Each one lets them judge your method before committing to a regular flow of work. And each one is easy to describe, which matters more than it sounds. "Give them one search and compare it with yours" travels much further than "they're a good IP firm."

I cover how to design an offer like this in [how to productise your service](/blog/how-to-productize-your-service). The principle is simple. A stranger can only buy what they can understand.

## A Marketing Routine for a Niche Field

![A small weekly routine that keeps running through busy delivery months](/images/blog/marketing-for-ip-firms/inline-3.webp)

An IP firm's market is small. You are not trying to reach millions of people. You are trying to be known by a few thousand IP managers, patent attorneys and R&D heads in your chosen field. That is good news, because a small market can be reached with a small, steady routine.

Here is where I would start:

- **One useful piece a week**, published where your buyers already look, usually LinkedIn. A search technique, a filing trend in your technology, a common mistake in how searches are briefed. Written in the name of a senior person in the firm, because a person's name opens more doors than a company logo; I explain the balance in [founder brand vs company brand](/blog/founder-brand-vs-company-brand).
- **Five conversations a week** with past clients, patent attorneys and people who once asked for a quote and went quiet. Not pitches. A short note about something relevant to them, such as a new filing trend in their field. IP firms sit on years of goodwill and almost never use it. [Cold outreach for consultants](/blog/cold-outreach-for-consultants) covers how to do this without sounding like a sales script.
- **One published landscape a quarter** in your chosen technology, built from public data. It is your case study, your content and your reason to call people, all at once.

Keep it small enough to survive a busy month. Delivery deadlines do not move, so marketing is always the thing that gets dropped. A routine with an owner who is not the founder, on a fixed day, keeps going when everyone else is buried in a batch of drafts.

None of this replaces referrals. Referred clients are usually your best clients. The point is to stop depending on them, so a quiet quarter is an inconvenience and not a crisis. I wrote about that shift in [why you should stop relying on referrals](/blog/stop-relying-on-referrals), and the wider system is in the [guide to getting clients beyond referrals](/client-acquisition-system).

## Questions IP Firm Founders Ask Me

### How do IP services firms get clients in India?

Mostly through referrals and repeat work: patent attorneys who trust your searches, IP managers who move companies and take you with them, and, for many Indian firms, law firms and corporations abroad who outsource search and drafting. Referrals stay important. The firms that grow steadily add two things: a clear specialisation that makes them easy to refer, and published work, such as landscapes and search methods, that brings buyers to them before a request arrives.

### How do you market a patent search firm when the work is confidential?

Show the method, not the client. Publish how you search, how you decide a search is complete, and what a good report looks like. Build patent landscapes from public data in the technologies you know and share them freely. And ask satisfied clients, in writing, whether you may describe the work without naming them.

### Is content marketing worth it for an IP firm?

Yes, if it demonstrates the skill you sell. A published landscape or a clear explanation of a search technique shows a buyer exactly what working with you would be like. Generic posts about "the importance of IP" do not, because every firm writes them.

### Should an IP firm hire a marketing agency?

Usually not first. An agency will ask what to say, and most IP firms do not yet have a clear answer beyond a list of services. Settle who you are for, what you are known for, and a small first engagement a stranger can say yes to. Then an agency, or an in-house marketer, has something worth promoting.

## An IP Firm That Only Grows by Introduction?

IP services firms do careful, expert work, usually under someone else's name, and they have always grown by introduction. That will not change entirely, and it should not. What can change is how much of the firm's future depends on it. A clear specialisation, a small first engagement a stranger can try, and a steady habit of publishing your method make you easy to find and easy to refer, before the next request lands on someone else's desk.
