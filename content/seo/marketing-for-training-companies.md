Picture the inbox of an L&D head at a mid-sized Indian company in the last week of March. The budget for the new year is about to be approved, and the training calendars have started to arrive. Forty of them, perhaps. Communication skills. Leadership essentials. Time management. Negotiation. Presentation skills. Each one a well-designed PDF with a list of modules, a photograph of a trainer in front of a flip chart, and a line about "transformational learning experiences".

Now imagine trying to choose between them. You cannot. They describe the same topics in almost the same words, and nothing in them tells you which trainer will change anything for your sales managers. So you do what any sensible person does with forty similar options. You keep the two or three you have used before, ask procurement to compare day rates on the rest, and move on to your next meeting.

That inbox is where most corporate training firms lose work, long before anyone has seen them train. This page is my guide to how to market corporate training: why it is harder than it looks, who actually makes the decision, the trap that turns good trainers into day-rate vendors, and the marketing strategy I use with training and coaching firms in India.

## Why Corporate Training Is Hard to Market

Training firms are built by people who are good in a room. That is the whole business: a trainer who can hold forty managers for two days and send them back slightly different. Clients notice, participants give warm feedback, and for a while the work arrives on its own. An L&D head who liked a programme books it again next year. A participant who was promoted remembers you and calls.

Then the pattern shows its limits. One large account accounts for half the year's work, and its L&D head changes jobs. A new HR team arrives with a new vendor list. The firm wants to sell into a different industry, where nobody has heard its name. And it finds it has no way to make work appear. It can only wait for the next call.

The sales symptoms at that point are very familiar to anyone in this business. Conversations with HR that go well and then drift. "Please send us your training calendar." A request to quote for a two-day programme, followed by a counter-offer at a lower day rate. Long empanelment forms that lead to nothing. Firms respond by sending more proposals and chasing harder, as if the problem were in the follow-up.

It usually is not. By the time a buyer asks for your calendar, they have already filed you under a topic, and a topic is something you compare on price. That is a marketing problem: what a buyer understands about you before they ever speak to you.

## Who Actually Buys Corporate Training

Part of what makes corporate training lead generation difficult is that the person you talk to is rarely the person with the problem.

There are usually three people involved, and they want different things.

**The problem-owner.** A sales head whose new managers cannot run a team meeting. A plant head whose supervisors are losing people. A CEO who has noticed that her senior team cannot present a plan to the board without reading slides aloud. This person feels the pain, and they are the one who will notice whether the training worked.

**L&D or HR.** The person who runs the training calendar, manages vendors, and has to defend the training budget every year. They want programmes that land well, are easy to schedule, and will not embarrass them. They are often your first contact, and they are almost never the person whose problem you are solving.

**Procurement.** Arrives at the end with a rate card and a vendor form. Their job is to make sure the company is not overpaying for something it could buy elsewhere. If your programme looks like something it could buy elsewhere, they will treat it that way.

Most training firms market only to the second person. Their brochures, their LinkedIn posts and their proposals speak the language of L&D: modules, learning objectives, competency frameworks. That is understandable, because L&D is who answers the email. But it means the problem-owner never hears from you directly, and L&D is left to argue for your programme on your behalf, in a meeting you are not in, against every other line in the budget.

The training firms that grow steadily find a way to reach the problem-owner as well, and they describe their work in the problem-owner's language: fewer people leaving in the first year, managers who can run a review, a senior team that can hold a room.

## The Topic Trap: Why Good Trainers Get Compared on Day Rate

![A training firm's topics filed in a pile, and one programme lifted out as a named outcome](/images/pages/marketing-for-training-companies.webp)

The deepest marketing problem in corporate training is that most firms sell a topic. "Communication skills." "Leadership development." "Sales training." A topic is a category, and a category has a going rate. Once a buyer has decided you are a communication skills trainer, you are being compared with every other communication skills trainer they know, and the easiest thing to compare is the fee.

I have seen this decide engagements more than skill ever did. One trainer I worked with was genuinely gifted. Participants loved him, and his feedback forms were the kind other trainers would frame. Winning corporate work was still a grind: long conversations with HR, budgets that never quite appeared, and a day rate far below what he was worth.

His craft was not the problem. His category was. "Communication skills training" sits in the mind of an HR buyer as a commodity aimed at the broad middle of the organisation, where budgets are thinnest. So we changed two things. We aimed the programme at senior leaders instead of the middle, and we renamed it for the outcome: Speak Like a Leader. Leadership development sits in a different budget, with a senior sponsor and far less resistance when HR takes it upstairs. Conversations that had drifted started closing, the engagements grew, and the fees moved into a band the old name could never have supported. He taught almost exactly what he had always taught. The full story is in [from communication skills to speak like a leader](/case-studies/cs-04-communications-trainer).

The lesson applies to most training firms I meet. You are not competing on quality, because the buyer cannot see quality from a brochure. You are competing on the shelf you have been put on. Change the shelf, and the comparison changes with it. I go deeper into this in [the praise trap](/blog/praise-trap-admired-but-not-paid), which is about firms that are admired and still not paid well.

## How to Market Corporate Training: Four Parts

![Four parts that turn a training topic into a programme a buyer can sponsor](/images/pages/marketing-for-training-companies-parts.webp)

The marketing strategy I use with training companies has four parts. They map onto the [CLEAR method](/clear) I work with, but the parts matter more than the names.

**A position built on a problem and a group.** Not a topic, but a problem for a particular group of people. "First-time managers in IT services who have just been promoted from the team they now lead." "Plant supervisors in manufacturing firms with high first-year attrition." "Senior leaders who have to present strategy to boards and investors." Narrowing like this feels risky to a trainer who can teach many things. You are not refusing other work. You are choosing what buyers remember you for, so that when the problem appears, your name comes with it. [Positioning for consulting firms](/blog/positioning-for-consulting-firms) applies to training firms almost word for word.

**Proof of change, not proof of delivery.** Most training firms publish proof that they delivered: photographs of a full room, a count of participants, a list of client logos. None of that tells a buyer whether anything changed. Proof of change is different. What were managers doing before, and what did they do three months later? What did the sales head notice? Even a short before-and-after, told honestly and without naming the client, does more than a hundred feedback forms rating the trainer 4.8 out of 5.

**Presence where the problem-owner looks.** The problem-owner does not browse training calendars. They read things about their own problem: how to keep new managers, why reviews go badly, how to prepare a leadership team for a hard year. A training firm that writes about those problems, in the trainer's own name, is the one a business head already knows when they ask L&D to find someone.

**A first step a stranger can take.** A full programme for fifty managers is a large decision for a company that has never worked with you. A small, defined first step lets a buyer test you without betting on you: a short paid needs assessment, a half-day session for the leadership team, a pilot with one cohort. I explain how to design one in [how to productise your service](/blog/how-to-productize-your-service), and how to price it in [value-based pricing for consultants](/blog/value-based-pricing-for-consultants).

Most training firms have one of these at best, usually a vague version of the first. Put all four together and the conversations change. Buyers arrive asking when you can start, not what your day rate is.

## Content and LinkedIn for Training Companies

Content is where training firms have a natural advantage they rarely use. A trainer spends days every month in rooms full of managers from different companies, hearing the same problems in different words. That is better material than most consultants ever get.

Marketing for corporate trainers works best when it shows what you see in those rooms. A few examples of the kind of content that does this:

- **A leadership trainer** writing about the three things new managers get wrong in their first performance review, and what to do instead.
- **A sales trainer** describing the objection that every team in a particular industry fumbles, and the one question that usually handles it.
- **A communication trainer** taking apart a real, public presentation by a business leader, and showing what made it land or fall flat.
- **A soft-skills firm** publishing what changed for one cohort three months after a programme, with the client's permission and without the client's name.

Notice that none of these are about training. They are about the problem-owner's world, which is exactly why the problem-owner will read them.

LinkedIn is the main place this lives for B2B training in India. It is where L&D heads and business leaders will look you up before they reply to anything. Post in the trainer's own name, once a week, about something you saw in a room. Avoid the common habit of posting only workshop photographs and certificates; they tell a buyer you were busy, not that you were good. The balance between the trainer's name and the firm's name is in [founder brand vs company brand](/blog/founder-brand-vs-company-brand).

Search is the quieter channel. Buyers do type their problems into Google: how to train first-time managers, why do new managers fail, how to improve presentation skills for senior leaders. A firm that has written a clear, honest answer to those questions meets buyers at the moment they are looking. The numbers are small, which suits a training firm that needs a few dozen good clients a year. [SEO for consultants](/blog/seo-for-consultants) covers how to approach it.

## Corporate Training Lead Generation in India: Where the Work Comes From

It helps to be honest about where training work actually comes from, because the answer shapes what is worth doing.

In my experience, most corporate training firms in India get the bulk of their work from four places: repeat bookings from L&D teams that liked them, past participants who have since been promoted or moved companies, the founder's own network, and vendor lists they have been added to over the years.

Two of those four deserve far more attention than they get.

**Past participants are your best future buyers.** A manager who sat in your programme five years ago may now be a business head with a budget, in a different company, facing the same problem you helped with before. Most training firms lose touch with participants the day the programme ends. A firm that stays in occasional, useful contact with them builds a list of future buyers who already trust it.

**L&D heads move.** When an L&D head who rated you highly joins a new company, they bring their vendor list with them. Knowing when that happens, and getting in touch soon after, is one of the cheapest ways to win a new account.

Vendor empanelment is the other two's opposite. It feels like progress, and it is necessary to get paid in many large companies, but it rarely decides who gets chosen. Being on a list of forty approved vendors puts you back in the inbox we started with.

There is also the calendar to respect. Many Indian companies plan training budgets in the first quarter and run most programmes between April and December. A firm that only markets when its trainers are free will always be visible at the wrong time. The planning conversations happen months before the programme, which is why the routine I describe below matters.

## How It Changes by Type of Training

The four parts hold across training and coaching. What changes is the problem, the buyer and the trap.

**Corporate training firms** with a wide catalogue usually suffer most from the topic trap, because a long menu is the hardest thing to position. The way out is to pick one or two problems and become known for them, even while the catalogue stays available. The full guide is [how to get corporate training clients](/blog/get-clients-for-corporate-training).

**Communication skills and public speaking trainers** face the commodity problem most sharply, as the case above shows. They also have an advantage: a talk at an industry event is a live demonstration of the product. The trap is treating the talk as the end. A speaking slot that leads to no follow-up conversation is a performance, not marketing.

**Leadership development firms** sell into a crowded promise, where every brochure says "transformational" and buyers have been disappointed before. Proof of change matters more here than anywhere else, along with a clear point of view about what good leadership looks like in a particular kind of company.

**Executive coaches** sell a relationship one person at a time, often to a buyer who will not talk publicly about having a coach. For them, the founder's own visibility and a small number of strong referral partners carry most of the weight.

## A Weekly Routine That Survives Training Season

![A small weekly routine that keeps running through busy delivery months](/images/pages/marketing-for-training-companies-rhythm.webp)

The biggest threat to marketing in a training firm is the training itself. When the trainers are delivering, nobody is marketing. When the season ends, the firm realises there is nothing in the pipeline for the next quarter, and everyone starts chasing at once. It is feast and famine with a calendar attached.

So the routine has to be small enough to run in the busiest delivery month of the year. Here is the shape I start with:

- **One post a week** in the lead trainer's name, drawn from something that came up in a room that week.
- **Five conversations a week** with past participants, past L&D contacts who have moved, and business heads who once showed interest. Not pitches. A short note about something relevant to them.
- **One proof story a quarter**, written up from a programme that has finished: what was happening before, what changed, what the sponsor noticed.
- **A fifteen-minute review each Monday** of every open conversation: what happens next, and who does it.

That is two to three hours a week, owned by someone who is not on stage that day, held in the calendar like a client session. It looks almost too small to matter. It matters because it keeps running through the busy months, which is exactly when most training firms stop. The wider system is in the [guide to getting clients beyond referrals](/client-acquisition-system), and [how to stop relying on referrals](/blog/stop-relying-on-referrals) goes deeper on the shift itself.

## What Marketing Consulting for a Training Company Looks Like

Since it is the obvious next question, here is how I work with training firms. I run workshops myself, so I know the strange economics of a business where the product is a person's time in a room.

I start with a diagnosis rather than a plan. The [Pipeline Reality Check](/pipeline-reality-check) is a one-week look at where your work actually comes from: your last twenty engagements, who brought them, what problem the sponsor was trying to solve, and which conversations went nowhere and why. It tells us whether the constraint really is marketing.

Sometimes it is not. If most enquiries convert and your trainers are fully booked, the answer is pricing or hiring, and I will say so.

When it is marketing, the work follows the four parts above: a position built on a problem and a group, proof of change in public, presence where the problem-owner looks, and a first step that is easy to say yes to. Then a weekly routine your team can run without me. Each engagement is scoped after a conversation, because a solo trainer and a firm with thirty facilitators need different things.

## Excellent in the Room, Invisible Outside It?

Training firms live on the quality of what happens in the room, and that will always be the heart of the business. What can change is how much of that quality a buyer can see before they ever book you. A position built on a problem, proof that something changed, presence where the problem-owner looks, and a small first step will move you out of the brochure pile and into the conversations where budgets are decided.
